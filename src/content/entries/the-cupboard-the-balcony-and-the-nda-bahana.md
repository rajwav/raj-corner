Aditya and I had spent Classes 11 and 12 together at Kendriya Vidyalaya Berhampur, while Shakti was off in Bhubaneswar studying at Akash. We were great friends, and as anyone who has been in that phase of life knows, any excuse to get out of the house and spend a few uninterrupted days together was worth taking.

The excuse happened to be the NDA exam.

Calling it an exam trip would be an insult to serious aspirants everywhere. Aditya and I weren't even remotely prepared. The exam was merely the *bahana*—the cover story that got us permission to pack our bags and go stay together.

***

### The Cupboard Operation

We were staying in a room and doing exactly what three teenage boys with zero exam pressure would do: playing cricket inside the room. No textbooks, no formulas, just indoor cricket and chaos.

Right in the middle of our match, heavy footsteps sounded outside, followed by a sharp knock on the door. It was the room owner.

Panic instantly set in. For reasons that made complete sense in that split second of teenage terror, Aditya and I decided our only salvation was a ridiculously small wardrobe cupboard inside the room.

Somehow—defying every law of geometry and human anatomy—both of us crammed ourselves inside that tiny cupboard. Shakti locked the door from the outside to make the room look totally innocent.

Seconds later, the owner walked in.

We were folded in half inside the dark cupboard, barely daring to breathe. Every single rustle felt like a siren. We could hear the owner walking around the room, talking to Shakti. One minute passed. Then two. Then three. The air was running out, our knees were jammed into our chests, and our limbs were cramping up.

He stayed for what felt like an eternity—about five excruciating minutes—before stepping away for a moment. The second we had a brief window, we managed to scramble out of the cramped wooden box, gasping for air. But we couldn't stay in the room; if he circled back, we were finished.

Our only escape route was the balcony.

***

### Twenty Minutes on the Balcony

We slipped out onto the balcony and ducked down in the shadows.

It was late at night, pitch dark, and there was essentially only one exit point. If the owner walked onto that balcony, there was nowhere to run. He would catch us red-handed, complain straight to our parents back home, and the entire NDA expedition would collapse in catastrophic shame.

My heart was pounding against my ribs. I was genuinely terrified.

And right then, crouched beside me in the dark, Aditya started muttering under his breath:

> *"Jai Shri Ram, Jai Shri Ram, Jai Shri Ram…"*

It was desperate, rhythmic, and intensely earnest. Seeing him chant, I didn't hesitate—I joined in right beside him:

> *"Jai Shri Ram, Jai Shri Ram…"*

There we were, two KV boys crouched like fugitives on a third-floor balcony late at night, softly chanting to the universe to keep the room owner from taking three steps outside. And strangely enough, amidst the sheer absurdity of the moment, that chant made the fear a little less unbearable.

We stayed completely frozen out there for roughly twenty minutes.

Eventually, footsteps faded, the door clicked shut, and the coast cleared. The owner had left without ever stepping onto the balcony.

We waited a minute to be sure, crept back inside, looked at each other... and within minutes, went straight back to playing cricket like none of it had ever happened.

***

### The Result

A few days later, we actually showed up to write the NDA exam. As expected from two candidates whose primary preparation consisted of cupboard acrobatics and balcony survival, we were completely clueless.

When the results came out, we found out we had scored exactly two marks.

Two.

Not twenty. Just two marks.

We didn't pass anything, we didn't qualify for anything, and we certainly didn't join the armed forces. But looking back, that trip gave us something far more enduring: twenty minutes on a dark balcony, a cupboard that should never have fit two people, and a friendship story we'll probably still be laughing about decades from now.
